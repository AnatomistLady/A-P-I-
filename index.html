A. Physiological Step Sequencing: Excitation-Contraction Coupling & Cross-Bridge Cycling (Questions 34–41)

Instructions: Place the sequential events of somatic motor excitation, electrical conduction, calcium release, and cross-bridge cycling in chronological order from Step 1 (Earliest) to Step 8 (Final Step)[cite: 15, 17, 18, 19, 22].

Step # Physiological Event Description
34. [   ] A somatic motor action potential arrives at the axon terminal, triggering exocytosis of acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft[cite: 15].
35. [   ] ACh binds to ligand-gated receptors on the motor end-plate, causing Na⁺ influx and producing local depolarization[cite: 17].
36. [   ] Voltage-gated sodium channels open along the sarcolemma, propagating the action potential into the transverse tubules (T-tubules)[cite: 18, 19].
37. [   ] T-tubule depolarization triggers terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm[cite: 18, 19].
38. [   ] Ca²⁺ binds to troponin, causing tropomyosin to shift away from the active myosin-binding sites on actin[cite: 19].
39. [   ] Energized myosin heads bind to actin to form a cross-bridge[cite: 22].
40. [   ] ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi) release from the myosin head, driving the power stroke that slides actin toward the M line[cite: 22].
41. [   ] A new ATP molecule binds to the myosin head, triggering cross-bridge detachment from actin[cite: 22].
Check Answers: Action Potential & Cross-Bridge Sequencing (34–41)

The correct chronological sequence is 34 → 35 → 36 → 37 → 38 → 39 → 40 → 41[cite: 15, 17, 18, 19, 22].

C. Muscle Energetics, Contraction & Fiber Types Matching (Questions 50–57)

Physiological Concept Description & Mechanism
50. Creatine Phosphate (CP) A. Isotonic contraction where tension is maintained as the muscle shortens and the joint angle decreases[cite: 37].
51. Anaerobic Glycolysis B. Rapid high-energy phosphate reservoir that regenerates ATP via creatine kinase for ~15 seconds of intense work[cite: 26, 28].
52. Slow Oxidative (SO) Fibers C. Contraction where internal tension builds but muscle length and joint angle remain completely unchanged (e.g., maintaining posture)[cite: 38].
53. Fast Glycolytic (FG) Fibers D. Non-oxygen-requiring catabolism of glucose producing 2 ATP and pyruvate/lactic acid, powering 1–2 minutes of heavy effort[cite: 27, 28].
54. Concentric Contraction E. Sustained, smooth contraction produced when stimulation frequency is so high that the relaxation phase is eliminated[cite: 40].
55. Eccentric Contraction F. Red, mitochondria-dense, myoglobin-rich fibers utilizing aerobic respiration for long-term fatigue resistance[cite: 32].
56. Isometric Contraction G. White, large-diameter fibers loaded with glycogen that contract rapidly but fatigue quickly[cite: 32].
57. Complete (Fused) Tetanus H. Isotonic contraction where tension diminishes as the muscle actively lengthens while joint angle increases (e.g., lowering a weight)[cite: 37].
